# 02-01 So you want to be a hedge fund manager?

## 1. Overview

The course assumes that our goal is to become a portfolio manager. 

A portfolio manager is responsible for managing investment funds, making investment decisions, and generating returns for investors.

A portfolio manager's motivation is often financial compensation. However, compensation depends on two main factors:

- 1. **Type of fund managed**: Different funds have different investment strategies and compensation structures.
- 2. **Fund performance**: How well the fund performs can directly affect the manager's compensation.

## 2. Type of funds

Three broad categories of investment funds that a portfolio manager might manage:

1. Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs)
2. Mutual Funds （Often require flat initial minimum investments, commonly ranging from $1,000 to $3,000）
3. Hedge Funds (is a private, actively managed investment pool that collects capital from wealthy individuals and institutional investors)

They differ mainly in how they are traded, how transparent they are, and how easily investors can withdraw their money.

- ETFs: Highly transparent and easy to trade.

- Mutual funds: End-of-day trading with periodic holdings disclosure.

- Hedge funds: More private investment strategies, less transparency, and potentially significant withdrawal restrictions.

### 8.1. Trading Algorithm – Execute Trades

The trading algorithm compares two portfolios:
- Target portfolio: What stocks and quantities the fund wants to hold.
- Live portfolio: What the fund currently holds.

It sends buy and sell orders to move the live portfolio toward the target portfolio.

Why not execute everything at once?

Large orders can move stock prices and result in poor execution. Trading algorithms therefore split large orders into smaller transactions, sometimes taking several days to complete a position.

### 8.2. Portfolio Optimizer – Determine Target Portfolio

The portfolio optimizer determines how much of each stock the fund should hold.

Its inputs include:
- Price forecasts: Which stocks are expected to rise or fall.
- Historical data: Open, High, Low, Close, and Volume (OHLCV).
- Volatility and correlation: Measures of individual stock risk and relationships between stocks.
- Current portfolio: Existing holdings and potential transaction costs.

The optimizer balances expected returns and portfolio risk to generate the target portfolio.

### 8.3. Forecasting Algorithm – Predict Future Prices

A forecasting algorithm predicts stock prices or returns over the next N days (e.g., 5 or 10 days).

It often uses machine learning models trained on:

- Historical market data.
- Proprietary information.
- Other relevant investment data.

The resulting forecasts are passed to the portfolio optimizer.

## 3. The order book

An order book is a record maintained by a stock exchange that lists outstanding buy and sell orders for a particular stock. It helps the exchange match buyers with sellers.

- Buyers submit bids.

### 3.3. What Happens When a Market Buy Order Arrives?

Suppose a buyer submits:

`Buy IBM, 100 shares, Market`

The exchange checks the order book and **finds the lowest available selling price: $100**.

It executes the transaction:

- Buyer receives 100 shares.
- Seller's remaining quantity decreases from 1,000 to 900.
- The ask price remains $100.

### 3.4. Important Concepts
- Bid: Demand to buy.
- Ask: Supply to sell.
- Order book: Collection of outstanding bids and asks.
- Matching: Exchange pairs compatible buy and sell orders.
- **Partial execution: An order may be filled in multiple transactions**.

The order book organizes buying and selling interest. Market orders execute against the best available prices in the order book, while unfilled limit orders remain there waiting for a matching order.
