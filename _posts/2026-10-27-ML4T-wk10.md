## 2. Grinold's Fundamental Law

<img width="1156" height="414" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/8ff1f6b3-fe80-4b92-854f-4a6e9c137129" />

[Portfolio Managment Research](https://www.pm-research.com/richard-grinold)

In the 1980s, Richard Grinold was seeking a method of relating performance, skill, and breadth in investing. He then developed a relationship he called the `Fundamental Law of Active Portfolio Management` where we could **combine skill and breadth to create an estimate of performance**.

<img width="730" height="449" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/d68a4c10-5768-460f-a2dd-ec7439a8e501" />

`The Fundamental Law of Active Portfolio Management`

- Performance = skill *  sqrt(breadth) or IR = IC * sqrt(BR)
  
Thus Two ways to improve performance:

1) improve skill;
   
2) more opportunities for applying that skill

- Breadth might relate to how many stocks you invest in, it is the number of trading opportunities.
- Skill relates to your skill in choosing them, is summarized in Information coefficient
- Performance is summarized by information ratio (IR)

### 3.3 The Key Question: How Should You Allocate Your Tokens?

Two possible strategies:

Strategy A: Concentrated Betting
- Bet all 1,000 tokens on one coin flip.
- Potential outcome: +1,000 or -1,000 tokens.
- Very high risk.

Strategy B: Diversified Betting
- Distribute tokens across many coin flips.
- Individual outcomes vary, but gains and losses can offset each other.
- Lower overall risk.

## 9. Information Ratio (IR)

### 9.1. What is IR?
The **Information Ratio (IR)** measures a fund manager's skill by evaluating excess returns relative to the risk taken to generate those returns.

It is essentially the Sharpe ratio of the skill component (alpha).

### 9.2. Decomposing Portfolio Returns

Portfolio returns consist of two components:

**The goal is to measure the manager's skill, not the return generated simply by following the market.**

### 9.3. Information Ratio Formula

<img width="658" height="202" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/8b1aa0a7-2b00-488d-be1d-9d0b8deba50f" />

Both are calculated using historical daily returns.

### 9.4. How to Calculate IR?

1. Calculate the portfolio's beta. (beta tells you how much a portfolio tends to move when the market move)
2. Calculate the market-related return for each day: \(\beta R_m\).
3. Subtract the market-related return from the portfolio return to obtain residual return (alpha).
4. Calculate the mean and standard deviation of the historical alpha values.
5. Divide mean alpha by its standard deviation.

## 10. Information Coefficient (IC) and Breadth (BR)

These are two components of the Fundamental Law of Active Portfolio Management:
IR = IC * sqrt(BR)

### 10.1. Information Coefficient (IC)
- IC measures the correlation between a manager's return forecasts and actual returns.
- It indicates how accurately the manager predicts which investments will perform well.
- A higher IC means better forecasting ability.

For example:
- Manager predicts IBM will increase by 1%.
- IBM actually increases by 0.5%.
- The forecast is directionally correct, contributing to a positive correlation.

IC generally ranges from:
- 0: No correlation between forecasts and actual returns.
- 1: Perfect positive correlation.

### 10.2. Breadth (BR)

Breadth represents the number of independent trading opportunities per year.

<img width="836" height="120" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/16f10614-1a5d-440f-a312-d9431e38eab0" />

**Important**: Even for a buy-and-hold portfolio, each stock position counts as a trading opportunity because breadth is measured on an annual basis.

Key takeaway
- IC = Forecasting skill: How accurately can the manager predict returns?
- BR = Breadth: How many investment opportunities does the manager have each year?

Higher forecasting accuracy and greater breadth can improve the Information Ratio.

# 02-10 Portfolio optimization and the efficient frontier

## 1. Overview

When you have identified a group of stocks that are good investments, the next question is:

**How much of your portfolio should you allocate to each stock?**

There are many ways to determine portfolio allocations. This lesson introduces `Mean-Variance Optimization (MVO)`, also called portfolio optimization.

Main objective
Given:
- A set of equities: The stocks available for investment.
- A target return: The desired expected portfolio return.
- Risk: The uncertainty or variability of portfolio returns.

**The goal is to: Find the allocation (weight) of each stock that minimizes overall portfolio risk while achieving the target return.**

## 2. What is risk?

In this lesson, risk is measured by **volatility**, specifically the **standard deviation of historical daily returns**.

Consider two stocks:
- Stock XYZ: Increased by 10% over the period but experienced large price fluctuations.
- Stock ABC: Also increased by 10%, but its price movements were much smoother.
Although both stocks achieved the same return, XYZ had higher volatility and therefore higher measured risk.

## 5. Can we do better?

<img width="1062" height="520" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/a2be4a3b-018b-4b63-b5d5-e0f38be602c1" />

The combined portfolio has its own overall risk and expected return.
- Portfolio return is determined by the weighted returns of its stocks.
- Portfolio risk depends on both individual stock volatility and how the stocks move relative to one another (correlation).

**Can we achieve high returns with relatively low risk by combining the right assets?**

### 5.1. Markowitz's key discovery: Covariance
Harry Markowitz (Nobel Prize winner) showed that portfolio risk depends not only on the individual risks of assets but also on how their returns move together.

This relationship is measured using covariance.
- Positive covariance: Two assets tend to move in the same direction.
- Negative covariance: Two assets tend to move in opposite directions.
- Low or near-zero covariance: Their movements are less related.

By combining assets with suitable covariance in the right proportions, investors can reduce overall portfolio risk.

Key insight
 A well-diversified portfolio can have lower risk than any of its individual assets.

### 5.2. Stocks and bonds: An example

Before Markowitz's discovery, many investors considered bonds the lowest-risk investment and believed that holding bonds alone was the best way to minimize risk.

Markowitz demonstrated that combining stocks and bonds can produce a portfolio with lower risk than holding either stocks or bonds alone, because their returns do not move perfectly together.

## 6. Mean-Variance Optimization

### 6.1. Markowitz's key insight: Combining assets with different correlations

Harry Markowitz recognized that portfolio risk depends on both individual asset volatility and how assets move relative to each other.

By combining assets with negative correlation (anti-correlation), investors can reduce overall portfolio volatility.

For example:
- Stock A increases in value.
- Stock B decreases in value.
- Their movements partially offset each other.
- The combined portfolio experiences less volatility.

This is the principle of diversification.

An ideal combination might have:
- Short-term: Low or negative correlation to reduce portfolio risk.
- Long-term: Positive expected returns so the portfolio can grow.

### 6.2. Mean-Variance Optimization (MVO)

Mean-Variance Optimization is an approach developed from Markowitz's Modern Portfolio Theory. It determines how to allocate money among assets to achieve a target return while minimizing risk.

**Inputs required by a mean-variance optimizer**

The optimizer considers four major inputs:

<img width="741" height="182" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/810fa750-5c3e-4fe6-a95b-fd5ae812ed61" />

Important distinction:
- Volatility measures the risk of an individual asset.
- Covariance measures how two assets move together.
- The covariance matrix contains the pairwise covariance values for all assets.

### 6.3. How does the optimizer work?

The target return can be selected between the expected return of the lowest-return asset and the highest-return asset (assuming a long-only portfolio).

The optimizer then:
1. Takes the expected return of each asset.
2. Considers individual asset volatility.
3. Evaluates relationships between assets using the covariance matrix.
4. Searches for the optimal allocation weights.
5. Finds the portfolio with the minimum risk while achieving the target return.

## 7. Efficient Frontier

The Efficient Frontier is a central concept in Harry Markowitz's Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT). It represents the portfolios that offer the highest expected return for a given level of risk, or equivalently, the lowest risk for a given expected return.
