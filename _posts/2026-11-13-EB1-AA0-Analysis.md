## Case1: APR162019_01B2203
### 1. Case background and overall outcome
The petitioner was a research scientist specializing in natural language processing and speech communication.

His academic and professional background included:
- Ph.D. in Applied Informatics (2007).
- Experience as a research assistant professor.
- More than 60 scholarly articles.
- Research publications in professional scientific journals.
- Participation in international conferences.
- Peer-review activities for professional journals.

The Nebraska Service Center initially denied the EB-1A petition because the petitioner satisfied only two of the ten regulatory criteria, whereas at least three were required.

The two criteria accepted by the Director were:
1. Participation as a judge of the work of others.
2. Authorship of scholarly articles.

Denied for **original scientific contributions of major significance**

### 2. What USCIS requires

The AAO explained that a petitioner must establish two separate elements:
1. The contributions must be original.
2. The contributions must be of major significance in the field.

The AAO explained that major significance may be demonstrated through evidence that the petitioner's research findings, original methods, or processes:
- Have been widely accepted and implemented throughout the field.
- Have remarkably impacted or influenced the field.
- Have otherwise risen to a level of major significance.

### 3. Evidence submitted by the petitioner

The petitioner relied on four major categories of evidence.

1. Scholarly publications
2. Citation history 
3. International conference presentations
4. Expert recommendation letters
5. Research grants and funding
   
### 4. Why AAO rejected the citation evidence

The petitioner argued that his publications had received approximately 360 citations.

However, the AAO identified two problems.

Problem 1: Reliability of the citation data

The petitioner submitted Google Scholar printouts but did not explain:
- How Google Scholar calculated the citation totals.
- How the citation information was generated.
- Whether the reported information was reliable.

The AAO therefore questioned the accuracy and reliability of the citation evidence.

Problem 2: Citation quantity did not establish major significance

More importantly, the AAO stated that even assuming the citation numbers were accurate, the petitioner had not demonstrated that the citations reflected widespread commentary or recognition of his work.

The AAO acknowledged that some researchers had cited and relied on his publications.

However, it found insufficient evidence that:
- His work was regarded as authoritative in the field.
- His research had significantly influenced the field.
- His findings had attracted widespread recognition.
- His research had generated substantial commentary or acceptance among other researchers.

The AAO specifically referred to USCIS policy guidance explaining that citation records may be probative when they demonstrate that an individual's work is cited as authoritative in the field.

Important distinction:

The AAO did not say that 360 citations were categorically insufficient for EB-1A.

It said that the petitioner had not demonstrated what those citations meant in terms of his actual influence on the field.

### 5. Why AAO rejected the expert recommendation letters

The petitioner submitted several letters from professors and researchers.

The letters described his research as:
- Groundbreaking.
- Cutting-edge.
- Interdisciplinarily important.
- Influential in acoustic modeling and speech synthesis.
- Important for advancing speech production systems.

One professor stated that the petitioner's work had helped move acoustic modeling forward by improving speech production systems and generating more natural, intelligible, and personalized speech.

Another expert described the petitioner's work as having gained considerable traction among researchers.

Why AAO found the letters insufficient

#### 5.1. The letters described research activities rather than demonstrating field-wide impact.

Many letters discussed:
- What the petitioner researched.
- What he published.
- Which conferences he attended.
- Why his research was technically valuable.

However, they did not adequately explain how his work had already influenced the field at a major level.

#### 5.2. The letters relied on general praise.

The AAO emphasized that letters using strong descriptive language without specific supporting evidence have limited probative value.

It cited USCIS policy guidance stating that letters lacking specifics and relying on hyperbolic language do not establish major significance.

#### 5.3. The letters did not establish widespread acceptance or authoritative influence.

The AAO acknowledged that some researchers had cited and relied on the petitioner's research.

Nevertheless, the letters did not establish that his work had become authoritative or significantly changed research practices across the field.

#### 5.4. The letters did not adequately explain the actual consequences of his contributions.

The AAO wanted evidence showing how his research had already advanced the field, rather than simply expert opinions that he was an outstanding researcher.

The AAO also cited Kazarian v. USCIS, noting that letters from physics professors attesting to a petitioner's contributions were insufficient without adequate evidence of their significance.

### 6. Why AAO rejected Research grants and funding

The petitioner also submitted evidence showing that he had participated in research funded by well-known entities.

One project involved research on the dynamics of vocal tract shaping. The petitioner subsequently published an article based on his research.

Why AAO rejected this evidence

The AAO noted that:
- The petitioner was involved in a funded research project.
- He was not specifically identified as a grant recipient.
- Receiving research funding does not automatically establish a contribution of major significance.

The petitioner needed to demonstrate either:
1. That the research funding itself reflected the major significance of his previous work; or
2. That the research conducted using the funding resulted in contributions of major significance.
Neither was established.

### 6. Incremental advancement versus major significance

The AAO acknowledged that the petitioner's work may have produced incremental advancements in the field. However, it explained that incremental advancement is expected in original scientific research.

The fact that research improves an existing model, technique, or scientific understanding does not automatically establish a contribution of major significance.

The AAO concluded:
- His research added to the general pool of knowledge.
- His publications demonstrated scientific productivity.
- His research had some recognition among other scientists.

But the evidence did not establish:
- Widespread commentary or acceptance.
- Recognition of his findings as authoritative.
- Significant influence on the field.
- A substantial advancement in the field attributable to his contributions.

The AAO also emphasized that recommendation letters repeating regulatory language without explaining how the petitioner's contributions had already significantly influenced the field were insufficient.
